# Advanced Design Lab — Sound Creation, Spatial Perspective and Foley Recording

Use this reference only when stock libraries cannot deliver the precise material, emotional contour, depth, or movement envelope needed; or when the user explicitly wants original-designed sounds. Do not turn a simple edit into unnecessary engineering.

## A. The five-component sound formula

Construct a high-impact but restrained effect from **orthogonal functions**:

1. **Causal source** — a meaningful contact/force event: paper strike, pen, hand cloth, gravel step, mechanism. Gives realism.
2. **Physical size** — controlled bass/low-mid body and resonant material, not just low-end volume. Gives mass.
3. **Velocity** — brief shaped broadband motion, proportional to on-screen acceleration and moving direction.
4. **Micro-detail** — fine snap/click/scrape that lets small speakers identify an event.
5. **World** — reflections, room/air, occlusion, and continuity; attaches the event to a place.

Not all five are needed. Start with **one great source**. Add a second layer only to fix a diagnosed perceptual absence. Check if layers compete at the same attack instant or pile up at the same spectral region. Build layer relationships using envelope and contrast, not simple summing.

### Design step sequence

1. Audition authentic source alone at intended mix level with narration.
2. Decide what is actually missing: motion? scale? detail? space? duration?
3. Select one complementary layer to supply the missing dimension.
4. Match attacks using listening and transient shape; offset slightly only with intention.
5. Shape tails separately. Fade or EQ each layer to avoid competing with VO.
6. Listen as a mono fold-down and on small speakers, without assuming sub-bass will survive.
7. Remove at least one layer if it adds no meaningful improvement.
8. Print/test the sound and retain original sources and license evidence.

## B. Foley design: record exactly what the picture suggests

Foley is often the cheapest path to an original premium sound, especially for close-up objects and tactile text/doc graphics. Record in a reasonably quiet room and monitor before a take. Choose the right material and perform naturally, not theatrical “animation foley.”

**Practical source ideas**:
- Paper card against paper board — gentle dry information reveal.
- Thin cloth sliding over notebook — airy close lateral motion.
- Finger on dense wood — soft marker/element “settle.”
- Soft pencil tap on paper — micro point or data reveal.
- Rubber sole on tile — controlled travel/body movement.
- Wallet, file folder, letter envelope — paperwork and banking sequences.
- Real luggage on floor — departure/airport visual authenticity when matched to shot.
- Key click or keyboard spacebar — tech interaction when actually indicated.

**Recordist protocol**:
1. Document the object, surface, space, mic, source distance and intended narrative cue.
2. Choose close perspective for isolated detail and medium/distant for context; avoid trying to transform a close dry click into a wide airport event with huge reverb alone.
3. Record 15–30 seconds of room tone where feasible; capture several natural takes with different speeds/intensities.
4. Leave quiet before/after each take for trimming and true tail observation.
5. Avoid clothing handling, breathing, cable movement, AC cycles, background announcements and automatic phone noises unless they are wanted.
6. Preserve raw masters, then high-pass/EQ or repair copies as necessary.
7. Do not remove all space from a realistic foley; sometimes tiny room reflections are the source of believability.
8. Test the chosen performance *against picture*, not only as a beautiful raw recording.

Phone recordings can be useful for quiet close Foley but need checking for noise suppression, compression artifacts, automatic gain control and stereo phase. An expensive studio microphone is not a prerequisite for good creative choices.

## C. Envelope physics and nonverbal storytelling

### Attack/decay language
- **Instant, short, damped** = tactile, small, decisive, intimate.
- **Slower attack, wide low-frequency swell** = anticipatory or evolving weight; may distract during voice.
- **Fast attack, long ring** = metallic/resonant event in a large or lively space; ensure material matches image.
- **Broadband noise that rises/falls with speed** = air/motion. Continuous bright noise can fatigue ears.
- **Quiet room with sudden absence** = attentional shift; silence can carry story without a synthetic hit.

### Easing mapping
For an animated object, compare x-position or camera motion over time; infer velocity. Choose a motion envelope roughly proportional to velocity, not absolute position. A particle may travel the farthest at the end but be decelerating: a whoosh that grows to the final frame will feel wrong. Start by drawing gain automation; only then consider spectral brightness, pan, or pitch mapping to velocity.

### Spectral hygiene
- Choose a **frequency seat** for each audible layer; avoid stacking bright 2–8 kHz attacks over sibilants and Nepali consonants. This is a broad listening principle, not a static EQ prescription.
- A believable ‘heavy’ impact often needs a controlled low-mid core with recognizable transient information; pure sub is inaudible on phones.
- Filter out low rumble from air textures if it competes with VO or exposes recording noise.
- Don't cut all bass from authentic footsteps, vehicle interior, or door Foley when weight is part of the event.
- Use a gentle de-esser/transient shaper only after trying better source, level, clip shape or narrower placement.

## D. Original sound design methods

### Varispeed and pitch
Slowing a small real-world sound can suggest larger scale, but it changes duration/formants and may introduce artifacts. Render high-quality source when possible. Test realism versus abstraction. If the sound clearly becomes a distorted “monster” effect, it probably violates a grounded documentary palette.

### Time-stretch
Use when a movement envelope needs a longer sustained body; avoid stretching the initial snap or end click until it smears. Often split transient/body/tail and stretch the body only. Comparison against natural takes is best.

### Reverse and pre-laps
A reversed soft air swell can suggest anticipation, but the familiar reverse-swish + giant impact formula is overused. Reverse short, organic material instead of a huge sci-fi effect; shape the release and fade long pre-roll before it distracts narration.

### Granular textures
Small recordings split into grains can create “data pressure” or gentle kinetic stepping. Keep grain attacks low, vary density slowly, and avoid a brittle metallic hiss. If the visual doesn't justify abstract texture, a truthful natural bed may be better.

### Synthesis
Use filtered noise for airy movement; a very short and controlled low-mid tone for a neutral settling accent; gentle modulated low-frequency texture for subtle scene tension; layered noise and soft transient for informational UI. Render with fades and avoid excessive loudness. Do not falsely describe a synthetic sound as authentic location recording.

### Convolution / room matching
A recorded clip can take on a space with an appropriate impulse response or short artificial room reverb. Before using one, ask if the footage really depicts the same space and distance. Long cathedral-style convolution under a small office close-up is misleading. Keep dialogue intelligibility and mono compatibility.

### Multiband dynamics / dynamic EQ
Consider only after simple level and edit fixes fail. Use it for short bright masking events, excessive low-end pulses, or adaptive beds around dialogue. Excessive processing creates timbral “holes,” pumping and unnatural motion; A/B at final output loudness.

### Organic motion via cloth
A thin soft cloth swish recorded from one close position, carefully trimmed and optionally low-pass filtered, can become a more believable parallax sound than stock “whip fast transition 03.” Variation comes from different performance speed and material, not simply repeating the same sample at changed pitch.

## E. Spatial image and 2.5D visual depth

**Front vs back:** meaningful close transients with little reflected content feel nearer; quieter/directness-reduced, soft reverb and ambience-linked elements feel farther. Alter each element coherently with its visual Z depth. Do not use exaggerated stereo widening as a replacement for distance.

**Left to right:** if physical movement is pictured, subtle panning can cue travel. When the motion is just text placement and not an object physically crossing space, center may be clearer, especially for mono phone speakers.

**Large movement vs tiny movement:** scale audible impact by perceived physical mass and camera speed, not literal pixel distance.

**Parallax still images:** a still photo moving in Z does not automatically make sound. The emotional storytelling sometimes works best with room tone that subtly “opens” as the camera approaches. An effect can imply that a still is physically moving, unintentionally cheapening an otherwise premium composition.

**Perspective change through cuts:** preserve the important acoustic continuity with J- and L-cuts. Cut a room tone *because the space changes*, not because the editor changed the picture.

## F. Constructed effect recipes with editable choices

### Recipe 1 — 200–500 ms restrained side reveal
- Primary: soft natural cloth/air swish with clean initial noise.
- Envelope: follow animation easing; decay before the next speech onset.
- Optional detail: delicate paper edge at actual foreground crossing.
- Processing: mild high-pass if rumble exists, attenuate distracting highs, minimal pan only if clear.
- Failure: too much stereo width or a giant tail over a 0.2-second graphic.

### Recipe 2 — hero-statistic “weight without trailer bass”
- Primary: small round low-mid impact or believable document contact.
- Optional upper texture: fine click from paper/card if the visual supports it.
- Envelope: brief controlled onset; no boomy two-second sustain.
- Failure: figure appears intellectually manipulative or words become inaudible.

### Recipe 3 — digital message without gamer UI
- Primary: one restrained actual message alert or phone-on-table vibration.
- Optional layer: fingertip touch/surface buzz for tactility.
- Avoid: three ascending synth notes, 8-bit beeps, long glitch distortion.
- Failure: the cue repeatedly plays although no messages arrive.

### Recipe 4 — quiet investigative pressure
- Primary: authentic room tone with faint fan/AC/traffic, perhaps filtered.
- Optional undercurrent: subtle slowly changing broadband or low tonal pressure, used sparingly.
- Transition: lower spectrum or narrow texture at emotional pivot without an obvious alarm.
- Failure: horror/true-crime trope over a measured informational NGO statement.

### Recipe 5 — grounded paper evidence
- Primary: actual paper sheet/folder shift captured dry.
- Optional weight: subtle fingertip/table contact.
- Tail: leave tiny authentic room decay; gently lower during narration.
- Failure: giant cinematic slam unrelated to the document's weight.

### Recipe 6 — support and safety end card
- Primary: no new SFX.
- Optional ambient: stable soft environment, nonrhythmic and restrained.
- Mix: isolate digits/URL/helpline instructions and give readable hold.
- Failure: number entry gets game-like clicks and the viewer cannot remember the number.

## G. Final challenge: the sound-swapping test

Ask: if this cue could be swapped into a sports montage, cryptocurrency ad, horror teaser, or wedding reel with no noticeable loss, it likely lacks physical/story specificity. Search again for source authenticity or redesign the envelope. The best documentary effects are specific enough to feel like a *real moment*, even when carefully designed.
